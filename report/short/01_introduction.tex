\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The question.} A person holds a smartphone at arm's length and brings it to touch the nose, with the
left or the right hand (Figure~\ref{fig:gesture}). The task, set by the principal investigator, is to predict
which hand it was from the phone's motion sensors alone. A phone has two such sensors: the accelerometer, which measures
the phone's acceleration and also feels gravity, and the gyroscope, which measures how fast the phone rotates. Our
recording app is a web page, and a web page can read both sensors~\cite{w3c2025deviceorientation}, on our
participants' phones about \R{describe/rate_hz_median_whole} times per second. The answer should be a probability,
\pright{}, that the right hand moved the phone, and it should be calibrated: of the movements given 80\,\%, about
80\,\% should really be right-hand movements. Above all, it must work for people the model has never seen, since
that is how it will be used.

\paragraph{Why it is hard.} People differ in arm length, speed and style, and the model sees no movement of the
person it is tested on. Phones differ in their sensors and in how the browser reports
them~\cite{stisen2015heterogeneity}. The sensors measure along the phone's own axes, which turn with the way the
phone is held, so the same arm movement gives different raw signals when the phone is held upright or sideways. Most
importantly, the two arms are mirror images of each other, so simple descriptions of a movement, such as its speed,
its duration or the length of its path, are about the same for both hands.

\paragraph{Our approach in brief.} Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the steps from one movement to an answer. We built
a web app that guides a participant through many such movements, asking each time for the left or the right hand,
and records the two sensors. Each recording is then cleaned and expressed relative to gravity and to the direction
the screen faces instead of the phone's own axes, so that it no longer depends on how the phone sits in the hand.
Physics tells us where to look: because the arms are mirror images, the hand should show mainly in quantities that a
mirror reverses, above all the direction in which the phone turns about the vertical. Early in the movement the phone
should turn counter-clockwise (seen from above) in the right hand and clockwise in the left, and the recordings
confirm this. Since this one quantity is not enough on its own, we trained several kinds of models, from a simple
rule to a small neural network, and tested each one only on participants it had never seen. Finally, we checked that
the predicted probabilities are calibrated, and we combined several movements made by the same person with the same
hand into one answer.

\paragraph{Related work.} Several studies have detected the hand that holds or operates a phone, or a user's dominant
hand, mostly to adapt the screen to that hand. Goel et al.'s GripSense used the touchscreen, the gyroscope and the
vibration motor to recognise how the phone is held (84.26\,\% of the hand postures recognised
correctly, 10 participants)~\cite{goel2012gripsense}. Löchtefeld et al.\ recognised the hand used for the unlock gesture from the
phone's sensors during that gesture, with a nearest-neighbour classifier, which gives a new example the label of the
most similar recorded one (98.51\,\% for 12 participants, all right-handed)~\cite{lochtefeld2015handedness}. Guo
et al.\ recognised the operating hand from the finger's traces on the touchscreen, and changes of hand from the
accelerometer and the gyroscope (about 94\,\% of the detections correct, 14 participants)~\cite{guo2016operating}.
Nelavelli and Ploetz used a single number that, like the turn of our phone, reverses between the hands: the direction
in which a swipe on the screen curves (99.53\,\% agreement with the hand, 12 users)~\cite{nelavelli2018adaptive}.
Fernández et al.\ used touch measurements of 174 participants (a detection rate of up to 99.92\,\% after seven
interactions)~\cite{fernandez2021implicit}, Chen et al.\ the motion sensors with a neural network that reads the signal step by step (an LSTM;
from 89.2\,\% in real life to 97.4\,\% while sitting)~\cite{chen2023handedness}, and Singh and Singh the
accelerometer, gyroscope and orientation sensor with a classifier built from many decision trees (XGBoost; 85.04\,\%, with three possible
answers rather than two)~\cite{singh2025dominant}. These numbers are hard to compare with ours, because several of
these evaluations let the same person appear in both the training and the test data: Guo et al.\ and Fernández et al.\
split their data at random, Nelavelli and Ploetz held out no users, and for Chen et al.\ and Singh and Singh it is
unclear. A model can then recognise the person rather than the hand, which overstates the accuracy for new
people~\cite{little2017crossvalidation,chaibubneto2019subject}: in a review of sensor and smartphone studies, the median
reported error was 5.6\,\% with random splits and 13.0\,\% when whole participants were held out~\cite{saeb2017usecase}, and
even the movement that brings a phone to the ear can identify its owner~\cite{conti2011mind}. With few participants, an
estimate also has wide error bars~\cite{varoquaux2018cvfailure}. The closest work to ours is clinical: in the
finger-to-nose test of a neurological examination, Mohammadi-Ghazi et al.\ saw with wrist motion sensors that the wrist
rotates in opposite senses for the two hands (17 participants), but built no classifier~\cite{mohammadighazi2022ataxia}.
Finally, modern neural networks are often over-confident~\cite{guo2017calibration}, so a predicted probability must be
checked for calibration.
