\paragraph{Would demographics help?} The app does not know who is using it. We asked whether knowing a participant's
age, sex, height, handedness and usual phone hand would help if it did. In each LOSO round, a small second logistic
regression combined the final model's score with these five items, and we compared it with the same combination
without them. They did not help; they made the accuracy slightly worse (Figure~\ref{fig:demo}): it stayed the same
for \R{demo/main_same} of the \R{demo/profile_n_persons} participants, fell for \R{demo/main_worse} and rose for
\R{demo/main_better}, a mean change of \Rcib{demo/main_dacc} percentage points (\Rcib{demo/inter_dacc} when each item
could also make the model's output count more or less), probably because with only \R{final/n_train_persons} training
participants the combination fits chance differences between people that do not hold for a new person. On their own,
the five items predict the hand at chance level (\R{demo/covonly_acc}), because every participant used both hands: a
fixed personal trait cannot tell which hand made a given movement.

\paragraph{PD01: who, and what was recorded.} Here we ask how one person with Parkinson's disease moved in this task,
compared with the \R{x1/n_ref_persons} participants (the reference participants of Figure~\ref{fig:pd01}). No model
was trained, tuned or tested on PD01, and nothing here is a diagnosis. PD01, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:data}, is
decades older than the participants and recorded with the same app, remotely, seated and without supervision, in
\R{x1/case_n_sessions} short sessions (\R{x1/case_session1_minutes} and \R{x1/case_session2_minutes}~minutes, against
\R{x1/ref_session_minutes_min} to \R{x1/ref_session_minutes_max} for the participants) that covered only the start of
the protocol, with the phone upright. The app accepted \R{x1/case_n_accepted} of PD01's \R{x1/case_n_attempts} tries:
\R{x1/case_n_free} warm-up trials, not used (as for the participants), and \R{x1/case_n_primary} instructed movements,
all with the left hand and the phone upright, which are compared below (\R{x1/case_n_primary_natural} of them at
natural speed, which the measures of the reach use).

\paragraph{What differs.} Before looking at PD01's data we fixed \R{x1/n_prespecified} characteristics of the movement:
how still the phone was held in the \R{x1/pre_window_s}~s before the movement (the hold); how fast, long, far and
smoothly it moved to the nose (the reach); how much the phone wobbled, that is, rotated back and forth, at 3--7~Hz (the
band of the tremor typical of Parkinson's disease at rest) and at 7--12~Hz; how the reach changed over repeats; what
happened in the \R{x1/nose_window_s}~s at the nose; and the first-half yaw. Each was compared between PD01's average and
each participant's average over the same kind of trial. PD01 lies outside the range of all the participants compared
(all \R{x1/n_ref_persons}, or the \R{x1/seq_peak_speed_mv_nref} with enough repeats for the change over repeats) in
\R{x1/null_pre_lfl_case} of the \R{x1/n_prespecified} characteristics (Figure~\ref{fig:pd01}a, top). PD01's reach had a
much higher peak acceleration, \R{x1/peak_acc_x_med} times the participants' median (\R{x1/peak_acc_pd} against
\R{x1/peak_acc_med}~m/s$^2$; highest participant \R{x1/peak_acc_max}), and its wobble at 7--12~Hz was
\R{x1/app_rms_7_12_x_med} times the median (\R{x1/app_rms_7_12_pd} against \R{x1/app_rms_7_12_med} degrees per
second). Over the reach, the phone's tilt changed less than for any participant (\R{x1/tilt_change_pd}$^\circ$ against
a median of \R{x1/tilt_change_med}$^\circ$), and in all \R{x1/nose_no_return_ncase} trials PD01 had not yet lowered the
phone when the recording stopped. While holding the phone before the movement, PD01 swayed unusually little at slow
frequencies, which made the share of the hold's rotation at 3--12~Hz the highest of all, without more fast wobble than
the participants. These \R{x1/n_robust_prespecified} differences survive every check we made, for example judging each
participant on only as many trials as PD01 made, or leaving out the trials in which PD01's accelerometer may have
reached its limit (an accelerometer cannot report values above a maximum, so a stronger acceleration is cut off there).
The others do not: the peak turning speed, just above the highest participant's, the fall of the peak speed over the
repeats, the reach's net turn and the share of the reach's rotation at 3--12~Hz. Where a difference might be expected,
PD01 was within the participants' range (Figure~\ref{fig:pd01}a, bottom): the 3--7~Hz wobble while holding
(\R{x1/pre_rms_3_7_pct}th percentile), the duration of the reach (\R{x1/duration_s_pct}th), its smoothness and pauses,
and the hand cue that the models read, the first-half yaw (\R{x1/fhy_hand_pct}th).

\paragraph{Unusual, but not unique.} With \R{x1/n_ref_persons} participants to compare with, even an ordinary person
lies outside everyone else's range on a few characteristics by chance alone. We therefore asked every participant the
same question: in how many of the \R{x1/n_prespecified} characteristics does that participant lie outside the range
of the other \R{x1/n_ref_minus_one}, judged on as many trials as PD01 made? The answer is a median of
\R{x1/null_pre_lfl_ref_median}, from \R{x1/null_pre_lfl_ref_min} to \R{x1/null_pre_lfl_ref_max}, and
\R{x1/null_pre_lfl_n_ge} participants (\R{x1/null_pre_lfl_ge_persons}) reach PD01's count of \R{x1/null_pre_lfl_case}
(Figure~\ref{fig:pd01}b). We also tested each characteristic with the Crawford--Howell test, a $t$-test made for
comparing one person with a small group, corrected for testing \R{x1/n_prespecified} at once (which makes some look
extreme by chance): the smallest p-value, \R{x1/min_p_ch_prespecified} for the peak acceleration, became
\R{x1/min_q_ch} after the correction, so no difference is statistically significant on the scales we fixed in advance
(on a plain instead of a logarithmic scale, the peak acceleration and the reach's 7--12~Hz wobble would be). With one
person against only \R{x1/n_ref_persons} others such a test has little power, so this is weak evidence either way.
PD01 is therefore among the most unusual people we recorded, but not unique. Moreover, PD01's age and phone, whether
the arm rested on something while PD01 sat (no one watched the remote recording), and an accelerometer that may have
reached its limit in \R{x1/case_n_clip_accepted} of the \R{x1/case_n_accepted} accepted trials could explain the
differences as well as the disease; telling these apart would need several patients and age-matched controls.
